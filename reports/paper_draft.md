# Abstract

Sylhet in north-eastern Bangladesh is among the wettest regions of the country, yet next-day rainfall guidance for a single location is rarely benchmarked against strong, honest reference forecasts. # 1 Introduction

Bangladesh receives most of its rainfall during the summer monsoon, and the north-eastern Sylhet region is one of its wettest parts, with orographic enhancement along the hills that border the Meghalaya plateau [@shahid2010]. Daily rainfall drives flash floods in the haor wetlands, landslides and agricultural decisions in this region [NEEDS CITATION]. Short-range numerical weather prediction (NWP) remains the operational basis of such forecasts, but many applied studies ask whether data-driven models trained on local time series can provide useful next-day guidance at low cost [NEEDS CITATION].

Gridded reanalysis products make long, gap-free daily records available for any location. The NASA Prediction of Worldwide Energy Resources (POWER) service distributes daily meteorological variables derived mainly from the Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2 (MERRA-2) [@gelaro2017; @sparks2018]. MERRA-2 precipitation over land is corrected towards observation-based products [@reichle2017], and POWER also offers satellite precipitation from the IMERG product of the Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) mission [@huffman2020], whose performance over Bangladesh has been evaluated by @ahmed2021. Such data are attractive for machine learning, but they also carry risks that are easy to overlook: inhomogeneities in the target series, inputs and target taken from the same model, and evaluation protocols that leak future information.

Gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost], LightGBM [@ke2017lightgbm] and CatBoost [@prokhorenkova2018] are strong learners for tabular data, and recent deep architectures for time series, including the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) [@lim2021], TSMixer [@chen2023tsmixer] and the Time-series Dense Encoder (TiDE) [@das2023tide], are now easy to apply through libraries such as Darts [@herzen2022]. Published comparisons for rainfall often report high accuracy, but they frequently omit strong reference forecasts, use random rather than chronological splits, or tune on the evaluation period [NEEDS CITATION]. For a skewed, persistent variable such as daily rainfall, a forecast can look accurate while adding little to "tomorrow is like today, adjusted for the season".

This study therefore asked a deliberately narrow question: how much skill do classical and deep machine-learning models add to strong statistical baselines for next-day rainfall at one site in Sylhet, when every model uses identical inputs and splits and the test years are evaluated only once? The contributions are: (i) a leakage-tested, chronological benchmark of five baselines, ten classical models, several regression formulations and three deep sequence models for rain occurrence and amount; (ii) a final evaluation whose configuration was frozen before the test years were opened (an internal pre-registration, not a public registry) with paired block-bootstrap confidence intervals and Holm-corrected Diebold–Mariano (DM) tests [@diebold1995; @harvey1997]; (iii) ablations of features, training window, rain-day threshold, look-back length and rainfall source; and (iv) an analysis of where the forecasts fail, including a check against independent satellite rainfall.

# 2 Study area and data

## 2.1 Study area

The study location was the NASA POWER grid point at 24.9157° N, 91.832° E in the Sylhet region of north-eastern Bangladesh. The elevation reported by POWER, 103.47 m, is the MERRA-2 mean elevation of the 0.5° × 0.625° grid cell, not the elevation of a station. Sylhet has a pronounced monsoon climate with most rain between June and September and a dry winter [@shahid2010]. We used four seasons: pre-monsoon (March–May), monsoon (June–September), post-monsoon (October–November) and winter (December–February).

## 2.2 NASA POWER daily data

Daily data for 1 January 1981 to 31 December 2025 were downloaded from the POWER Agroclimatology community on 14 October 2026 (file SHA-256 recorded in the repository). POWER defines days in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC); because Bangladesh time is UTC+6, one POWER day covers 06:00–06:00 local time. The variables are listed below. Most come from MERRA-2 [@gelaro2017]; shortwave radiation is labelled Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System (CERES) SYN1deg in the file header, and IMERG_PRECTOT is satellite precipitation from IMERG [@huffman2020] even though the header describes it as MERRA-2 total precipitation [CHECK]. All variables are reanalysis or satellite estimates for a grid cell, not rain-gauge observations.

| Variable | Description | Units | Use |

| PRECTOTCORR | bias-corrected precipitation (MERRA-2) | mm/day | target and predictor |
| IMERG_PRECTOT | satellite precipitation (IMERG, 1998–2025) | mm/day | secondary target only |
| T2M, T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN | 2 m air temperature: mean, maximum, minimum | °C | predictors |
| T2MDEW | 2 m dew/frost point | °C | predictor (Set B) |

## 2.3 Data audit and cleaning

The raw file was never modified; a parsed copy only replaced the missing-value code −999 by missing values. Three problems were found and reported rather than silently corrected. First, every WD2M value from 31 December 2012 onwards was invalid (values between about −9,000 and −5,400), so WD2M was dropped. Second, IMERG_PRECTOT starts in 1998 and radiation in 1984, so these variables were excluded from the main feature sets. Third, PRECTOTCORR is temporally inhomogeneous, with step changes around 2000 and 2016 that are not present in IMERG (Section 4.1); this is consistent with changes in the observations used to correct MERRA-2 precipitation [@reichle2017] [CHECK]. A forward fill of interior gaps of at most two days was allowed, but no cell needed filling. The calendar columns YEAR and DOY were removed so that calendar year could not become a predictor of the 2016 regime.

# 3 Methods

## 3.1 Targets

The forecast for day t+1 was issued from information available at the end of day t. The primary classification target was rain on day t+1 of at least 1 mm (`y_cls`), the standard wet-day definition of the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI) [@zhang2011]; thresholds of 0.1 mm and 2.5 mm were sensitivity analyses. The regression target was the rainfall amount on day t+1 in mm (`y_reg`). Log-target models were trained on log(1 + y) and back-transformed with the smearing estimator of @duan1983, $\hat y = S \exp(\hat z) - 1$ with $S$ the mean of the exponentiated log-scale residuals, estimated on an out-of-sample inner slice.

## 3.2 Features and leakage tests

Two feature sets were built from day-t information only. Feature Set A (71 features) used PRECTOTCORR, T2M, T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN, RH2M and WS2M; Feature Set B (115 features) added PS, QV2M, T2MDEW, WS10M and the dew-point depression (DPD). <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | n_features_A=71; n_features_B=115 --> Feature families were current values, lags of 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 days (rain as log(1 + mm)), rolling means over 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, rolling log rain sums, wet/dry spell counters, one-day tendencies, the diurnal temperature range (DTR) and DPD, and day-of-year harmonics with the month; calendar year was never a feature. Rolling windows required complete windows, so the first 29 days were dropped, leaving 16,406 rows shared by both sets. <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | n_rows=16,406 --> Set B was used for all headline models; Set A was an ablation.

Leakage was tested automatically: (i) a truncation test recomputed the features after deleting all data after a random date and required identical feature values up to that date, with a deliberately leaky negative control that had to fail; (ii) the target had to equal the next calendar day's rainfall; (iii) no feature could equal the target; and (iv) a Spearman scan on training rows flagged any feature with |ρ| > 0.9 against the target. All tests passed; the largest correlation was 0.81, for today's rain. <!-- source: 02_leakage_correlation_scan_train.csv | index=rain_log1p | abs_max=0.81 --> <!-- source: 02_leakage_truncation_test.csv -->

## 3.3 Chronological splits

Rows were assigned to splits by the date of their target. Development used targets from 1981 to 2019, and the test period (targets 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2025) was locked behind a function that raised an error unless called with an explicit confirmation. Model selection used five expanding-window folds with training targets from 1981 up to 2004, 2007, 2010, 2013 or 2016 and validation on the following three years; a one-day purge removed the last training row so that no validation target was also the reference day of a training row (Fig. 2). Within each training fold, the last three target years formed an inner "slice" with the same purge; the slice was used to choose classification thresholds (maximum critical success index), early-stopping lengths and smearing factors, after which the model was refitted on the full training fold. The validation block was never used for any choice. Deep models used a single split (training targets 1981–2014, validation 2015–2019) because of their cost, with the 2012–2014 slice for early stopping. Scalers, imputers and encoders were fitted inside pipelines on training rows only. Random k-fold cross-validation was excluded; with random folds about 80 % of validation targets appeared as same-day rain predictors of training rows.

Before the test years were touched, the complete configuration was frozen in `final_config.yaml`: feature set, model list, hyper-parameters, seeds, per-seed thresholds, numbers of trees or epochs and smearing factors, all derived from an inner split of the development data (training targets up to 2016, slice 2017–2019). The final run refitted every model on all development targets up to 30 December 2019, predicted the 2,192 test days once, and wrote the predictions with a manifest of the code commit and configuration hash. <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | test_days=2,192 --> No model, threshold or test was changed afterwards.

## 3.4 Models

**Baselines.** Five baselines were fitted on training rows only: majority class / training mean; persistence (the empirical probability of rain given rain today, and "tomorrow equals today" for amounts); climatology (target day of year with a circular ±7-day window); a seasonal model (logistic or ridge regression on two day-of-year harmonics); and seasonal + persistence, which adds today's rain flag or amount to the seasonal model. Seasonal + persistence was the main reference because exploratory analysis of the development years showed that season and persistence are the two sources of easy skill.

**Classical models.** For classification we used logistic regression without penalty and with L2, L1 and elastic-net penalties, logistic regression trained by stochastic gradient descent (SGD), random forest, XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost], LightGBM [@ke2017lightgbm], CatBoost [@prokhorenkova2018] and a multilayer perceptron (MLP); for regression the same families with linear, ridge, lasso, elastic-net and SGD regression and an MLP with Huber loss. Linear models used one-hot month encoding and standardisation inside a pipeline; classifiers used balanced class weights. The MLP (two hidden layers) was implemented in PyTorch [NEEDS CITATION] because the installed scikit-learn MLP did not support class weights, robust losses or a user-supplied chronological validation set.

**Regression formulations.** Four formulations were compared during development: direct squared-error regression in mm; regression of log(1 + mm) with naive and smeared back-transformation [@duan1983]; a two-stage hurdle model (probability of a wet day from the CatBoost classifier multiplied by a CatBoost amount model fitted on wet days with log target and smearing); and Tweedie-loss boosting [NEEDS CITATION]. CatBoost Tweedie and the hurdle model were carried to the final evaluation.

**Deep sequence models.** TSMixer [@chen2023tsmixer], TiDE [@das2023tide] and TFT [@lim2021] were implemented with Darts 0.48.0 [@herzen2022]. Inputs were a 14-day look-back of the target and the nine other Set-B weather variables as past covariates, with day-of-year harmonics as known future covariates; the networks therefore learned their own temporal features. Rain occurrence was modelled with a Bernoulli likelihood; amounts were modelled in log space with smearing (TSMixer, TiDE) or with quantile regression at seven quantile levels from 0.05 to 0.95 (TFT, point forecast = mean of the quantile function). One-step forecasts were produced from observed look-backs. Each stochastic model was run with five seeds (0–4), and the seed-mean forecast was the tested forecast. We refer to all non-baseline models as learners.

## 3.5 Hyper-parameter optimisation

Classical models were tuned with Optuna [NEEDS CITATION] using the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) sampler (seed 42) and a median pruner applied to the running mean of the objective over the five folds. Objectives were BSS for classification and MAE for regression; the hurdle amount model was additionally tuned for the mean squared error skill score (MSESS). Budgets ranged from 15 trials (random forest) to 100 trials (SGD). <!-- source: 06_tuning_budget.csv | task=cls & model=random_forest | budget=15 --> <!-- source: 06_tuning_budget.csv | task=cls & model=sgd_logloss | budget=100 --> Because the best trial is selected on the same folds, the best parameters were re-run with seeds 1–4 to measure selection optimism. Deep models were tuned on the 2012–2014 slice only (20 trials for TSMixer and TiDE, 12 for TFT; one seed per trial) and then re-run with five seeds on the 2015–2019 validation years; the untuned default configuration was kept wherever tuning did not improve validation skill.

## 3.6 Verification metrics

Classification was scored with the Brier score and BSS, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (ROC-AUC), the area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC), the expected calibration error (ECE, 10 equal-width bins), and, at the frozen threshold, the probability of detection (POD), false alarm ratio (FAR), critical success index (CSI) and Heidke skill score (HSS). With hits $a$, false alarms $b$, misses $c$, correct negatives $d$ and $n = a+b+c+d$:

$$\mathrm{POD}=\frac{a}{a+c},\qquad \mathrm{FAR}=\frac{b}{a+b},\qquad \mathrm{CSI}=\frac{a}{a+b+c},$$

$$\mathrm{HSS}=\frac{2(ad-bc)}{(a+c)(c+d)+(a+b)(b+d)}.$$

For probabilities $p_i$ and outcomes $o_i$, $\mathrm{BS}=\frac1n\sum_i (p_i-o_i)^2$ and $\mathrm{BSS}=1-\mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Amounts were scored with MAE, root mean squared error (RMSE), bias, recall and precision for days of at least 20 and 50 mm, and $\mathrm{MSESS}=1-\mathrm{MSE}/\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The reference for BSS and MSESS was climatology fitted on the same training rows; skill against seasonal + persistence was reported in addition because the climatology reference degraded when the target changed between decades (Section 4.3).

## 3.7 Statistical testing

Uncertainty was quantified with a paired block bootstrap [NEEDS CITATION] using calendar months of the target date as blocks (1,000 resamples, seed 42); the same blocks were used for all models so that differences are paired. Pairs of forecasts were compared with the DM test [@diebold1995] on daily loss differentials (squared error of probabilities, absolute error and squared error of amounts), using a Bartlett heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) variance [NEEDS CITATION] with bandwidth $\lfloor 4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$ (7 for the 2,192 test days), the small-sample correction of Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold (HLN) [@harvey1997] and a t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. Two families were pre-declared: family A compared every model with seasonal + persistence, and family B compared every model with the pre-declared headline reference (LightGBM for the Brier score, CatBoost Tweedie for absolute error, the hurdle model for squared error). p-values were adjusted with the Holm procedure [NEEDS CITATION] within each family, task and loss, at α = 0.05. Development-stage and ablation intervals are uncorrected bootstrap CIs and are described as such.

## 3.8 Ablations, error analysis and interpretability

Ablations changed one factor at a time on the development folds: feature set (A versus B; solar radiation from 1984), removal of each feature family, class weights, training window (from 1998, 2000 or 2010 onwards), rain-day threshold (0.1, 1 and 2.5 mm), look-back length of the deep models (14, 30 and 60 days) and rainfall source (IMERG instead of PRECTOTCORR as target and rain predictor, 1998–2019). Error analysis on the frozen test predictions was descriptive and stratified by month and season, observed intensity, year, local weather state of misses and false alarms, and dry-to-wet transitions. Interpretability used development data only: grouped permutation importance by feature family and physical variable, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values from TreeExplainer [NEEDS CITATION], accumulated local effects (ALE) [NEEDS CITATION] with partial dependence plots (PDP) for comparison, standardised linear coefficients across folds, and the variable-selection network (VSN) weights of TFT. The coverage of the TFT prediction intervals on the test years (Section 4.5) was computed in the final reporting stage from the frozen test predictions; like the error analysis, it was descriptive and changed no model, threshold or test.

# 5 Discussion

The central result is that machine learning added a modest but reliable amount of probabilistic skill to a strong statistical baseline. The gain in BSS over seasonal + persistence of about 0.07–0.11 was significant for every classical model and for TFT and TSMixer (Holm-adjusted p ≤ 0.0001), whereas the differences among the good models were an order of magnitude smaller and not significant (Table 3). <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | famA_cls_learner_p_holm_max=0.0001 --> This pattern, where the choice between learners matters far less than the choice of a good reference, is a direct consequence of the data: next-day rainfall at one grid cell is dominated by persistence and season, and both are available to every model. Regularised logistic regression captured most of the gain, so a simple, interpretable model is a defensible operational choice when probability forecasts are the goal.

For amounts, no model beat seasonal + persistence significantly in MAE (the best, CatBoost Tweedie, had p_Holm = 0.160), and the ranking depended on the error measure. <!-- source: 07_statistical_comparison.csv | family=A & task=reg & loss=abs_error & model=catboost_tweedie | p_holm=0.160; significant=False --> Median-type forecasts (Tweedie loss) minimised absolute error but under-estimated amounts, while mean-type forecasts (hurdle with Duan smearing, TiDE and TSMixer in log space) were nearly unbiased and detected more heavy-rain days but had larger absolute errors. Reporting MAE, MSESS and bias together is therefore necessary; any single metric would have produced a misleading winner. The smearing correction was essential for log-target models [@duan1983].

Deep sequence models did not earn their computational cost here. TFT was not significantly different from the boosters for probabilities but cost more than an order of magnitude more training time and offered reasonably calibrated quantiles, TiDE was the weakest learner for occurrence, and TSMixer showed that a single unlucky seed can fail. With about 14,000 development days at one location, the data volume is small for networks with hundreds of thousands of parameters, and the tabular models already receive hand-crafted temporal features [NEEDS CITATION].

The most important caveat is the target. PRECTOTCORR is a corrected reanalysis product with documented inhomogeneities [@reichle2017], and the step change around 2016 made the test years wetter than the development years. Skill measured against climatology was inflated by this change, which is why we report skill against seasonal + persistence as the headline. More fundamentally, the frozen forecasts retained discrimination but lost almost all probabilistic skill when scored against IMERG. Because predictors and target come from the same reanalysis system, part of the measured skill may reflect internal consistency of MERRA-2 rather than predictability of observed rainfall. Studies that use POWER rainfall for machine learning should therefore validate against independent rainfall, ideally rain gauges of the Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) [NEEDS CITATION], and IMERG evaluations for Bangladesh [@ahmed2021] suggest that satellite products are themselves imperfect references.

The error analysis explains where the skill comes from. The models were most useful in the transition seasons, when rain is neither certain nor rare, and least useful in the core monsoon. Heavy rain was damped and lagged by a day, the signature of an adjusted-persistence forecast without information about approaching weather systems. Pre-monsoon convective storms (nor'westers) and other advected systems cannot be anticipated from local surface variables alone [NEEDS CITATION]; upstream fields or NWP output would be needed.

# 6 Limitations and future work

1. **Reanalysis target.** The target is MERRA-2 PRECTOTCORR for a 0.5° × 0.625° grid cell, not rain-gauge data, and predictors and target come from the same system; skill against IMERG was close to zero. Future work should evaluate against BMD gauges in Sylhet and against IMERG as a primary target. <!-- source: 08_limitations_summary.csv -->
2. **Single grid cell.** All 115 predictors are local; no spatial or upstream information was used. Multi-cell or gridded inputs and convolutional or graph models are natural extensions.
3. **Inhomogeneous target.** PRECTOTCORR has step changes around 2000 and 2016; we documented but did not correct them. Homogenisation or training on IMERG would test robustness.
4. **Seasonal imbalance.** Almost all monsoon days are wet, so within-monsoon discrimination rests on few dry days (31 in the test monsoon seasons).
5. **Heavy rain.** Extremes were under-predicted and lagged; quantile or extreme-value losses and NWP inputs are needed for warning applications.
6. **Sample size for deep models.** 14,214 development days are small relative to the 412,927 parameters of the tuned TFT classifier; deep-model tuning did not transfer between periods. <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | n_dev_rows=14,214; tft_cls_tuned_parameters=412,927 -->
7. **Hindcast, not an operational system.** No NWP inputs were used, and the forecasts were not compared with operational NWP or BMD forecasts, which are the relevant baselines for practical use; POWER days are UTC rather than a local gauge reporting day; and POWER data are published with a latency of weeks, so day-t inputs are not available in real time.
8. **Non-stationarity.** Test errors were larger and skill against climatology higher than in development; shorter recent windows did not help.
9. **Single test period and multiplicity.** One six-year test window was evaluated; development-stage contrasts and the 118 ablation contrasts were not corrected for multiplicity, and tuned cross-validation scores carry selection optimism.
10. **Interpretability.** Collinear predictors limit attributions to groups of variables; importance does not imply causation.

# 7 Conclusion

Using a leakage-tested, chronological protocol frozen before testing, we found that gradient-boosted trees, regularised logistic regression and TFT all provided significantly better next-day rain probabilities for Sylhet than a seasonal + persistence baseline (Holm-adjusted p ≤ 0.0001), with no significant differences among them, <!-- source: 09_derived_numbers.csv | * | famA_cls_learner_p_holm_max=0.0001 --> while no model significantly improved the absolute error of amount forecasts. Deep sequence models did not justify their cost, and the skill largely disappeared against independent satellite rainfall. Honest baselines, frozen configurations and independent verification data are therefore at least as important as the choice of algorithm for data-driven rainfall forecasting from reanalysis data.
